<h2>How to execute on crazy ideas well</h2>

<p>The talk explored how an organization can build a culture where people share, encourage, and contribute to ideas - then turn the best of them into experiences that actually work. I broke that process into a few practical principles:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Protect the basics.</strong> An ambitious side project should never put the event's essential logistics, safety, or infrastructure at risk.</li>
  <li><strong>Pick an idea you love.</strong> Enthusiasm is the fuel that keeps a project moving, and teams do their best work when organizers can champion ideas they genuinely care about.</li>
  <li><strong>Distill the idea.</strong> Define the feeling you want to create, the reason participants will care, a clear North Star, and the smallest version that delivers the experience.</li>
  <li><strong>Make it easy to say yes.</strong> A concise pitch should explain the participant journey, the help required, the major risks, and a realistic backup plan.</li>
  <li><strong>Own the outcome.</strong> Ownership is not doing every task yourself; it is taking responsibility for whether the final result works across the whole team.</li>
  <li><strong>Deliver the experience.</strong> Prototype early, create a deadline before the deadline, test with real users, and polish both the visible journey and the invisible details around it.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Capture the Flag as a case study</h2>

<p>I used <a href="/projects/programming/ctf/">Capture the Flag</a>, a project I led for Hack the North 2025, as the talk's main case study. Its three pillars were a skills-based acceptance path, accessible challenges, and rewarding fun over pure competition. Those principles gave us a way to make decisions about difficulty, progression, rewards, and recognition without losing sight of the participant experience.</p>

<p>The project also showed how to keep a crazy idea responsible. We isolated it from Hack the North's critical infrastructure, spread the work across a mixed team, tested every challenge end to end, and kept one clear owner for the final experience. The result reached 1,337 players and reinforced one of the talk's central ideas: simple and excellent beats impressive and broken.</p>
